The printing press changed Europe by dramatically increasing the capacity to reproduce and circulate written and visual information. Before the mid fifteenth century, most European books were copied by hand, which made production relatively slow and expensive. Mechanical printing allowed printers to create multiple copies from the same typeset text, reducing the marginal labour required for additional copies. The technology did not immediately make books cheap or universal, but it changed the economics of publishing and gradually expanded the availability of written material. Printing shops emerged across European cities, producing religious works, classical texts, legal documents, schoolbooks, calendars, pamphlets and commercial information. The printing press was therefore not merely a faster way to make books; it altered the infrastructure through which European societies produced, preserved and distributed knowledge.
