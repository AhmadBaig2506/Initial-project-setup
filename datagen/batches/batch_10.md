Tornadoes form when powerful thunderstorms develop rotating air that becomes concentrated into a narrow, rapidly spinning column extending toward the ground. They most often occur when warm, moist air near the surface meets cooler, drier air above it, creating an unstable atmosphere. Strong changes in wind speed and direction with height can then help a thunderstorm develop rotation, while rising air currents may stretch that rotation and intensify it. When a rotating column of air extends from the storm to the ground, a tornado has formed. Although tornadoes are often associated with dramatic funnel clouds and violent winds, their development involves several distinct processes. Atmospheric instability supplies energy to thunderstorms, wind shear helps organize their rotation, and powerful updrafts reshape that rotation within the storm.
